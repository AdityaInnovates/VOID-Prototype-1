# Why 8 GB Macs cannot stretch to 9B

**Short answer: no, not usefully, and VOID should stay on MLX Swift with Qwen3.5-4B 4-bit as its deep brain.** llama.cpp's memory-mapping lets a larger-than-RAM model load and run, but on 8 GB it turns every token into SSD reads. The only 8 GB data point found, Qwen3.5-9B Q3_K_M (4.4 GB) on an 8 GB A18 Pro MacBook Neo, produced **3.9 tok/s generation and 7.8 tok/s prompt processing**, and commenters blamed memory pressure ([r/LocalLLaMA](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rr197e/snapshots/2026-03-13T23%3A00%3A09.503181Z)). In the same ballpark, an MLX blogger's 9B never finished a 256-token run in 5 minutes on a **16 GB** M3 Air, while the 4B did 9.8 tok/s in 2.6 GB ([HN mirror](https://hackernews-dynamic.seasondane.workers.dev/news/48358776)). Colibri does not help: it streams Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) experts from NVMe, needs the dense part of the model resident, lists 10 GB minimum RAM for its smallest MoE (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B), and has no 8 GB Mac benchmark ([README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri)). MLX has no upstream equivalent of llama.cpp's mmap paging; the third-party layer-streaming projects measured under 0.6 tok/s on 8 GB devices. The honest caveat is that **almost none of this was measured on an 8 GB M3**, so Section 5 lists the five measurements that would settle it in an afternoon. If you want something larger, the one experiment worth running is an opt-in "think harder" mode with a 9B at 3-bit in llama.cpp, with VOID's other models unloaded, and only if your own numbers beat roughly 5 tok/s with green memory pressure.

## mmap trades load time for first-token time, and 8 GB leaves no slack

llama.cpp memory-maps the GGUF file by default, so "loading" takes milliseconds and the real cost moves into page faults during the first tokens. Tensors are pointers into the mapping, and the pages are clean file-backed pages that the kernel can drop and re-read, so they are never written to swap ([DEV explainer](https://dev.to/multigrid/llamacpps-mmap-and-mlock-flags-explained-43fp)). This is a blog description, not a benchmark, and I could not verify its claim that `--mlock`/`--no-mmap` are being replaced by a `--load-mode` flag. `--mlock` pins pages so they cannot be evicted, which is exactly wrong for an 8 GB machine that also runs macOS, a menu-bar app, speech-to-text and text-to-speech. One guide advises keeping model, KV cache and OS overhead under about 70% of unified memory before using it ([Hannecke](https://medium.com/@michael.hannecke/tuning-llama-cpp-on-apple-silicon-843f37a6c3dc)).

The larger-than-RAM behaviour follows from the mechanism, and I found no 8 GB measurement of it. When weights exceed available page cache, evicted weight pages are re-read from the SSD on later tokens, so throughput slides toward SSD speed instead of crashing. That is the safer failure mode for SSD wear, since read-only weights add read traffic but no writes; wear risk comes from anonymous memory (KV cache, compute buffers, other apps) being swapped, and from `--no-mmap`, which copies weights into heap memory that must itself be swapped. By inference mmap is therefore the right default on 8 GB. The only wear evidence found is a 2021 report of M1 Macs using roughly 10% of rated write endurance in two months, self-reported and suspected to be swap-driven ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/apple-macs-with-m1-chip-reportedly-suffering-excessive-ssd-wear-in-some-cases)); nothing quantifies LLM inference specifically.

GPU offload (`-ngl`) does not behave as it does on a discrete GPU. With unified memory there is no second pool to spill into, so partial offload mostly moves compute between CPU and GPU rather than saving RAM; I found no source showing partial `-ngl` reduces resident memory on Metal. The GPU working-set cap is stated as about **75% of RAM** by the llama.cpp maintainer ([discussion #4167](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/4167)), measured at exactly 49152 MB on a 64 GB M2 ([issue #2048](https://github.com/ggerganov/llama.cpp/issues/2048)). That implies roughly 6 GB on an 8 GB Mac, but no source gave the real figure (other notes cite 5.3-5.8 GB, unverified), and old 8 GB M1 reports show failures once a 7B q4 model was forced fully onto the GPU. A rule of thumb from another discussion puts macOS at 3-4 GB, leaving about 4 GB for you ([discussion #25065](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/25065)). Raising `iogpu.wired_limit_mb` needs sudo, resets on reboot, and on 8 GB only starves the OS, so it is not a shippable lever.

Quantization is the lever that does move the needle, and the arithmetic is unforgiving. Qwen3.5-9B GGUF sizes are Q8_0 9.53, Q6_K 7.46, Q5_K_M 6.58, **Q4_K_M 5.68**, IQ4_XS 5.17, **Q3_K_M 4.67** and UD-Q2_K_XL 4.12 GB, against 2.74 GB for the 4B at Q4_K_M ([techplained](https://techplained.com/qwen-3-5-gguf-quantization)). Unsloth's mean KL divergence from full precision rises from 0.019 at Q4_K_M to 0.032 at Q3_K_M and 0.097 at Q2_K_XL, though the page does not label the model size ([techplained](https://techplained.com/qwen-3-5-gguf-quantization)). On Llama 3.1 8B, Q3_K_M costs 6.4% in perplexity and Q2_K 29% ([DEV](https://dev.to/jeff_geiser/q80-isnt-slow-because-of-swap-287p)); small models usually degrade faster, which is my expectation rather than a sourced result. KV cache is not the problem: Qwen3.5 is a hybrid with few full-attention layers, so the KV cost is a few hundred MB at 8-16K context, and the earlier VOID research found about 32 KB per token for the 4B. Compute that KV cost against Qwen3-8B, which needs 1.2 GB at f16 for 8K and 4.8 GB for 32K ([llmhardware.io](https://llmhardware.io/guides/kv-cache-quantization-ollama-llama-cpp)), and the case for Qwen3.5 over older 8B models on this machine is clear. Speculative decoding is out too: a draft model needs its own weights in the same RAM, and mlx-swift-lm cannot speculate on Qwen3.5 at all because its recurrent caches are not trimmable (finding from the earlier VOID report).

## Colibri solves a different problem: giant MoE models on big-RAM machines

Colibri (JustVugg/colibri, Apache-2.0, first seen around July 2026, about 47.6k stars) is a pure-C engine, one file per model family, that keeps a model's dense weights in RAM and streams routed experts from NVMe ([README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri)). Its flagship case is GLM-5.2: 744B parameters but about 40B active per token, with roughly **9.9 GB of int4 dense weights resident** and 21,504 experts (about 370 GB) on disk. The tiers are hot experts on GPU, warm in RAM, cold on NVMe; a per-layer LRU cache plus a learned pinned "hot set"; one `pread` per expert; a loader pool overlapping reads with compute; and router-lookahead prefetch, since GLM-5.2's routing is 71.6% predictable one layer ahead. Mirroring the model on a second NVMe added 37.5% decode speed, and a compressed MLA KV state stores 576 floats per token instead of 32,768 ([README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri)). These techniques only pay off when a model is sparse: they do nothing for a dense Qwen3.5-9B, where every weight is touched on every token.

Colibri's own numbers confirm it is a big-memory tool. Its README states a minimum of 8 GB RAM for the "smallest model", but its per-model table gives **10 GB minimum and 20 GB measured for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B**, 16/24 GB for GLM-5.2 and 32/64 GB for Kimi K3 ([README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri)). On Macs the Metal backend is experimental and opt-in (`COLI_METAL=1`); the documented results are on M4 and M5 Max machines with 128 GB or more, at **0.30 tok/s on CPU and 0.42 tok/s on Metal** for GLM-5.2 on an M4 Max, and 2.06 tok/s on an M5 Max with a 46.9 GB pinned expert set ([docs/metal.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JustVugg/colibri/main/docs/metal.md)). Independent reports are slower still: about 0.1 tok/s on 32 GB, and one tester saw looping, incoherent output ([HN mirror](https://hn.svelte.dev/item/48842459)). The small-model rows (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 104.8 tok/s on CUDA, OLMoE at 354.6) come from an RTX PRO 4000 desktop with 62 GB RAM, not a Mac ([README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri)). The project is also prototype-grade: open issues in September 2026 included a dropped end-of-sequence token that made generation non-terminating and a converted container missing weights for 36 of 78 layers ([Issues](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/issues)), and an earlier macOS RAM-detection bug collapsed the expert budget to 1 GB until v1.11.0 ([release notes](https://newreleases.io/project/github/JustVugg/colibri/release/v1.11.0)). It has no stable C API, only a CLI and an OpenAI-compatible server (`coli serve`).

The transferable lesson is the technique, not the code. A Metal proof-of-concept expert-slot cache for llama.cpp measured Qwen3-30B-A3B Q6_K at 38.1 tok/s with all experts resident (27.2 GiB), 27.5 tok/s with 64 slots (16.1 GiB), 15.7 tok/s with 16 slots (7.8 GiB) and 12 tok/s with 8 slots (6.3 GiB) on a 36 GB M3 Pro ([discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)). Even the smallest configuration there wants 6.3 GiB wired, more than an 8 GB Mac can spare, and it is not mainline llama.cpp. A separate SSD-streaming prototype on a 16 GB M1 measured 6.06 GB/s with double-buffered reads and projected about 6.3 tok/s for Qwen3-30B-A3B ([discussion #27149](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/27149)), but end-to-end speed on real compute was not reported. My extrapolation is that a 30B-A3B MoE on 8 GB would be SSD-bound at low single-digit tokens per second at best; nothing measured supports more. A 35B MoE also still needs the full file on disk (17 GB or more) and offers no clear quality advantage over the 4B once quantized hard enough to fit.

## MLX has the knobs for tuning, but no escape valve for oversized models

MLX does not mmap safetensors by default. A third-party analysis found its loader calls `pread()` into a freshly allocated buffer, and an mmap patch (OptMLX) gave mixed load times, under 3% throughput difference across eight Qwen3 builds, and in two of eight configurations a much higher peak (Qwen3-8B-4bit rose from 4.72 to 8.82 GB) ([OptMLX](https://atomgradient.github.io/OptMLX)). An upstream feature request for mmap-based out-of-core streaming, aimed squarely at users with 8-16 GB, exists as issue #2878, but I found no maintainer reply or implementation ([mirror](https://gh.r-mj.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/2878)). So in MLX a model that exceeds the budget swaps or fails, with no paging safety valve. Setting `Memory.memoryLimit` does not help much, since it defaults to 1.5 times the device's recommended working set and is only a soft guideline ([Memory.swift](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift/main/Source/MLX/Memory.swift)).

What mlx-swift does give is cheap and worth using: `Memory.cacheLimit` (the docs say even 2 MB caches perform well for many workloads), `Memory.clearCache()`, `Memory.snapshot()` for active, cache and peak bytes, and a wired-memory ticket system in mlx-swift-lm. On the KV side, `maxKVSize`, `kvBits` and `kvScheme` exist, and the source notes that state-space/GDN layers do not consume the `maxKVSize` budget ([Evaluate.swift](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/main/Libraries/MLXLMCommon/Evaluate.swift)). Those settings shave tens to hundreds of MB, not the 2-3 GB gap between a 4B and a 9B.

The third-party offload projects confirm the ceiling. MLXLayerStream loads one layer at a time from NVMe; on an **8 GB iPad Air M3 and iPhone 15 Pro Max it ran Qwen3.5-9B-6bit at 0.27-0.28 tok/s** fully streamed, 0.45-0.57 tok/s with 14-20 of 32 layers resident, 0.03 tok/s when over-allocation caused swap thrash, and the fully resident 9B needed 7.2 GB and crashed the iPad ([paper](https://atomgradient.github.io/MLXLayerStream/paper.pdf)). Its Mac Studio numbers show the same shape: 68.3 tok/s fully resident versus 1.7 tok/s fully streamed for the 9B-8bit. These are the authors' own measurements on iOS-class devices, not an 8 GB Mac, but the iPad M3 shares the chip family. MLX-Flash reports big wins only for MoE (Qwen3-30B-A3B at 82.6 tok/s versus 3 for a swapping Ollama on a 36 GB M3 Max) and parity for dense Qwen3-8B (53.5 versus 51 tok/s), recommending 16 GB or more ([MLX-Flash](https://github.com/szibis/MLX-Flash)). SwiftLM's `--stream-experts` ran a 122B-A10B model at 3.8-4.3 tok/s with 12.7 GB RSS on a 64 GB M1 Max, but its reviewer saw zero SSD I/O in the metrics, so the streaming claim was unverified ([lilting.ch](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/swiftlm-m1max-hands-on)). My conclusion: for a dense 9B on 8 GB, bandwidth math puts SSD-streamed decode near 0.5 tok/s, unusable for a voice assistant that must start speaking within a second or two.

MLX 4-bit builds do not rescue a 9B either. mlx-community's Qwen3.5-9B-MLX-4bit is about **5.6 GB** (5.06 bits per weight including non-text tensors) ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/Qwen3.5-9B-MLX-4bit)), the same class as llama.cpp's Q4_K_M. MLX now supports 2-6 bit affine quantization and 1-bit on Metal as of v0.32.4, but I found no quality numbers for 3-bit or mixed Qwen3.5-9B builds ([mlx releases](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/releases)).

## Adding llama.cpp to VOID is an engineering bet on grammar, not on size

Integration is easy. The official llama.cpp release publishes an XCFramework with every build (daily cadence, b12002-b12017 on 30 October), and `mattt/llama.swift` (MIT, Swift 6.0+, macOS 13+) wraps that prebuilt binary and re-exports the C API ([releases](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/releases), [llama.swift](https://github.com/mattt/llama.swift)). `tattn/LocalLLMClient` exposes llama.cpp, MLX and Apple Foundation Models behind one Swift API but calls itself experimental ([repo](https://github.com/tattn/localllmclient)). Metal is on by default in macOS llama.cpp builds ([build.md](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/docs/build.md)). My inference is that a thin `LlamaEngine` actor over the C API, pinned to an exact version and kept behind a backend protocol with the existing MLX engine, is the least-risk shape; VOID's swap would touch model download (GGUF instead of safetensors), chat templating, token streaming, and unload logic, not the whole app. Notarization, sandbox entitlements and XCFramework size were not verified, and the LFM2 first-supported build was not pinned down, though Liquid AI publishes llama.cpp-ready GGUFs ([LFM2-1.2B-GGUF](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-1.2B-GGUF)). Qwen3.5 support exists in llama.cpp (the hybrid gated-delta-net operator landed early in 2026, per a [downstream changelog](https://hex.pm/packages/llama_cpp_ex/0.6.0/files/CHANGELOG.md)), and Qwen3.5-9B Q4_K_M is a 5.3 GB download in one guide's listing ([Hermes guide](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/guides/local-llm-on-mac)).

The performance trade is real but runs against moving. On an M4 Max, MLX decodes faster than llama.cpp: Qwen3.5 2B at **291.9 versus 149.7 tok/s**, Gemma 4 E4B at 113.5 versus 80.5, and uses 10-28% less peak memory (Qwen3.5 2B 1223 versus 1443 MB), while llama.cpp wins time to first token on short prompts (29 versus 42 ms for the 2B) ([bench repo](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)). A separate M5 Max test of Qwen3.5-9B found llama.cpp at 67 ms TTFT versus 289 ms for MLX through an omlx server, but 70 versus 96 tok/s decode, and the author recommended llama.cpp for 8-16 GB machines ([Hermes guide](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/guides/local-llm-on-mac)). That guide's MLX figure goes through a server, so its TTFT may include overhead. Neither source ran an 8 GB M3, and mlx-swift-lm 3.32.3 has since added Qwen3.5 decode speedups (compiled traces, windowed prefill), so the gap may be smaller now. On the bandwidth-bound M3 (about 100 GB/s) the decode gap will plausibly narrow, but that is unmeasured. Running both runtimes in one process doubles Metal buffer pools, and with a 2-3 GB model each that is the opposite of what 8 GB needs.

The one place llama.cpp offers something MLX Swift does not is constrained decoding. GBNF grammars and JSON-schema constraints are first-class there, which could make a temperature-0 `tool_id {json}` router unable to emit malformed output, though the grammar docs list limitations (no `prefixItems`, broken nested `$ref`, performance pitfalls) and the C-API sampler path was not verified ([grammars README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/grammars/README.md)). mlx-swift-lm has `LogitProcessor` hooks but no verified grammar equivalent. That is a reason to spike llama.cpp for the router, if malformed tool calls are a real problem in your logs, and not a reason to migrate chat.

## Recommendation, and the five measurements that would overturn it

**Decision: stay on MLX Swift. Keep `Qwen3.5-4B-4bit` (3.03 GB per the HF tree) as the deep brain and a ~1.2B LFM2 as the reflex, with `Qwen3.5-2B` as the fallback, exactly as in the earlier VOID model report.** Do not adopt Colibri, do not integrate llama.cpp for size reasons, and do not use the MLX layer-streaming forks. The evidence is lopsided: the only 8 GB-class 9B datapoint is 3.9 tok/s and swap-limited, the 9B on a 16 GB Air in MLX did not finish, and every streaming approach on 8 GB hardware measured under 0.6 tok/s. Qwen3.5-4B already decodes at about 32 tok/s on an M3 and has the best small-model emotional-intelligence number found previously, so the quality gap to a 3-bit 9B is smaller than the speed gap. A 3-bit 9B also gives up exactly what small models need most; the cliff at Q3 and below on a 9B is unmeasured, but Q3_K_M's KL divergence is already 1.7 times Q4_K_M's.

Two optional follow-ups are justified. First, a **grammar-constrained router spike** using llama.swift and a ~1.2B LFM2 GGUF, worth doing only if your router mis-formats tool calls. Second, an **opt-in "deep think" mode** that unloads reflex, TTS and STT, loads Qwen3.5-9B Q3_K_M or IQ3/IQ4_XS through llama.cpp with default mmap, no `--mlock`, and quantized KV, and treats it as a batch-style feature (slow answers, shown on screen) instead of a conversational mode. Gate it on your own measurements below.

Because 8 GB-specific evidence is almost absent, run these on your Mac before building anything:

| # | Measure | How | Decision it drives |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Real GPU cap | Print `device.recommendedMaxWorkingSetSize` (Metal) at startup; also the llama.cpp startup log | Whether 5.3, 6 or another GB figure is the true budget; sizes every other choice |
| 2 | 9B Q3_K_M and IQ4_XS in llama-bench, mmap on | Run `llama-bench` on both with `-ngl 99` and `-ngl 0`, fresh boot, then again with Safari/Xcode open; log tok/s and memory pressure | Whether 9B ever beats about 5 tok/s with green pressure; the opt-in mode is dead below this |
| 3 | Swap and wear | `vm_stat` and `sysctl vm.swapusage` before/after a 20-turn session; note Activity Monitor pressure color | Whether mmap'd weights are being evicted and re-read, and how much swap the KV/buffers cause |
| 4 | Cold vs warm TTFT | Reboot, run the first prompt, then repeat; compare mmap vs `--no-mmap` | How bad the first-token page-fault cost is on a 4-5 GB model |
| 5 | Same-model MLX vs llama.cpp on your chip | Qwen3.5-4B 4-bit MLX vs Q4_K_M GGUF: TTFT, decode tok/s, peak memory, 5 prompts x 3 runs | Whether the 1.4-2x M4 Max decode advantage of MLX holds on a bandwidth-limited base M3 |

Also confirm your 14-inch M3's GPU core count (8 or 10), because the earlier prefill figures came from 10-core machines.

## Conclusion

The shape of the answer changes the question: on 8 GB, the problem is not that large models cannot be loaded, but that no known technique makes them fast enough to talk to. mmap lets a 9B start, Colibri-style expert streaming works only because MoE models read a sliver of weights per token, and MLX layer streaming shows what happens to a dense model that must re-read most of itself from SSD for every token. All three routes are bounded by the same SSD-versus-RAM bandwidth gap, which is more than an order of magnitude on this class of machine.

The practical upside is that the useful frontier for VOID is not parameter count but memory discipline and prompt caching: a 4B plus a small reflex with a byte-stable cached prefix, a low `Memory.cacheLimit`, and a 2B fallback will beat a swapping 9B on the thing a voice assistant is judged on, which is response latency. The one thing llama.cpp could add that MLX cannot, grammar-constrained tool routing, is a small, separable experiment and is a better use of your time than chasing bigger weights.
