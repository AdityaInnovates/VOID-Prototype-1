# Fix VOID's routing before swapping its models

**The biggest, cheapest quality gains for VOID are probably not a new model but a better router, a better eval, and a few drop-in swaps; a new model is worth testing only after those exist.** Liquid AI's LFM3-1.7B (released 27 Oct 2026) is the most interesting newcomer: a 0.97 GB MLX 4-bit build with vendor-reported tool-calling and instruction-following scores above Qwen3.5-4B, and Hindi among its 16 languages. But every LFM3 number is vendor-reported, no independent evaluation existed as of 28-29 Oct, and mlx-swift-lm 3.32.3 almost certainly has no `lfm3` architecture yet, so it is a "test in Python first" candidate, not a swap. Qwen3.5-4B remains the knowledge and reasoning anchor, and no new small dense Qwen exists. Thinking is voice-viable only as an escalation path (hidden behind a filler phrase, hard-capped, same weights), and the evidence says it helps math-like multi-step problems, not general assistant quality. Of the inference tricks, an OptiQ mixed-precision 4-bit build of Qwen3.5-4B is a near-free quality upgrade, while MTP speculative decoding (1.09-1.20x on an M4 Pro) and KV quantization are marginal on this hardware. The best-evidenced specializations are a multilingual embedding router feeding retrieved few-shot examples, plus a LoRA fine-tune of the router trained on rented GPU time. The honest caveat: **nothing here was measured on an 8 GB M3, and no Hinglish benchmark exists for any candidate**, so the plan starts with a 150-300 command eval set that turns every claim below into a measurement.

## LFM3-1.7B leads on paper, but only on paper

The model field changed less than the headlines suggest. Qwen has shipped no new small dense model after 3.5; its 3.6 and 3.7 releases are 27B, 35B-A3B, Flash and API models ([llmgateway timeline](https://llmgateway.io/timeline/2026)). The real newcomer is LFM3, six open-weight ~2B models released 27 Oct 2026, of which the text model LFM3-1.7B uses a 24-layer hybrid of 13 gated short convolutions, 5 sliding-window attention and 6 global attention layers, with 256K context and Hindi in its 16 languages ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B)). The 4-bit MLX build is **973 MB** ([HF tree](https://huggingface.co/api/models/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B-MLX-4bit/tree/main)), smaller than Qwen3.5-2B-4bit (1.72 GB) and about 0.3 GB larger than the LFM2-1.2B reflex.

Liquid's own card reports IFEval 96.66 (Qwen3.5-4B 92.52), IFBench 73.03 (38.16), BFCL V4 71.39 (55.12, and 62.83 for Qwen3.5-9B) and ToolSandbox 80.36 (75.40), but GPQA Diamond 63.37 against 75.76 for the Qwen 4B ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B)). That pattern is exactly what a voice router wants and a chat companion fears: strong at following instructions and calling tools, weaker at knowledge, and the card itself says it is not recommended for complex multi-step reasoning and that knowledge tasks should use retrieval. The only third-party datapoint is a secondhand MacPaw report of 64% vs 48% for Qwen3.5-2B on 65 macOS agent tasks ([andrew.ooo](https://andrew.ooo/answers/what-is-liquid-ai-lfm3-open-weight-2b-models-2026/)), and one briefing notes the lead rests on a single evaluation ([runaihome](https://runaihome.com/blog/lfm3-local-ai-hardware-guide-2026/)). Speed claims (237 tok/s on an M5 Max, 43.6 on an iPhone 17 Pro) are also vendor-only. My reading is that LFM3-1.7B is a credible replacement for the 1.2B reflex and possibly a faster tool router, but not for Qwen3.5-4B's open-ended answers.

Two practical blockers apply. First, mlx-swift-lm 3.32.3 (30 Sep) predates LFM3, and its release notes mention Gemma 4, Qwen3.5 and LFM2/2.5 embedders but no `lfm3` ([releases](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/releases)); LFM3 ships custom modeling code and is a different hybrid from `lfm2`, so you need a newer release, a port, or an interim Python mlx-lm or llama.cpp (b11960 or newer) harness. That absence is inferred from release notes because the model directory could not be read. Second, thinking is on by default, and `enable_thinking=False` "can be inconsistent on reasoning-heavy tasks" ([model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B)). The card also recommends temperature 0.1 plus a repetition penalty of 1.1, and LFM3 emits XML tool calls where LFM2.5 emits Pythonic ones by default ([LFM2.5 card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-2.6B)), so VOID's temperature-0 parser and sampler need checking, including whether the Swift sampler exposes a repetition penalty (not verified).

The other candidates fall behind on fit. **LFM2.5-2.6B** has strong instruction-following numbers (IFBench 59.17 vs 48.40 for Qwen3.5-4B on Liquid's card) but "always thinks", which is a latency liability for a router ([LFM2.5 card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-2.6B)). **MiniCPM5-2B** posts BFCL v4 66.6, IFEval 86.7 and AIME25 86.5 against 78.8, but supports English and Chinese only, which rules it out for Hinglish ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/OpenBMB/MiniCPM5-2B)). **Gemma 4 E2B** has the broadest multilingual claim (35+ languages) and community praise for translation, but its MLX 4-bit build is **3.55 GB**, larger than Qwen3.5-4B-4bit at 3.03 GB; this corrects the earlier assumption that E2B "fits comfortably" ([HF tree](https://huggingface.co/api/models/mlx-community/gemma-4-e2b-it-4bit/tree/main)). Its tool-calling scores in Liquid's tables are poor (BFCL 36.98 for E2B), and early community reports of broken tool calling may be launch bugs that were later fixed, which I could not verify ([Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1sbec70/snapshots/2026-04-09T16%3A11%3A00.655487Z)). Independent data on Qwen3.5 remains the best-grounded: Artificial Analysis gives the 4B an Intelligence Index of 27, the 2B 16 and the 0.8B 9, with an 80% hallucination rate for the 4B ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/qwen3-5-small-models)). I did not investigate Phi, SmolLM3, Granite micro, Nemotron nano, Falcon-H1 or Hunyuan small, so "no better option exists" cannot be claimed.

## Thinking helps math, not chat, and only fits behind a filler phrase

Reasoning mode is a large win on verifiable multi-step problems and a small one elsewhere. LFM2.5-1.2B-Thinking beats its Instruct sibling by 25 points on MATH-500 (87.96 vs 63.20) and 21 on GSM8K, yet trails slightly on GPQA Diamond and IFBench ([model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Thinking)). For Qwen3.5-4B, the earlier report's EQ-Bench gain from thinking was only +4.5 (27.1 vs 22.6). The cost is 4-10x more tokens: Qwen3-8B emits about 4,400 tokens on MATH500 in thinking mode vs 958 without ([Demystifying Hybrid Thinking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.12680)). At roughly 30 tok/s (an assumed rate, not measured here) that is minutes, so only a hard cap of about 100-300 thinking tokens (3-10 s) is conceivable, and I found no measurement of capped-thinking gains on tool or chat turns at this size.

Control is leakier than the switch suggests. An external judge found implicit reasoning in 92-100% of no-think outputs from Qwen3-4B/8B/14B ([same paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.12680)); Qwen3.5 small models default to non-thinking and use the `enable_thinking` template flag ([Unsloth guide](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5.md)), but I have no leak data for them. Qwen's thinking budget works by cutting off the think block and inserting a stop-thinking sentence ([Qwen3 Technical Report](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09388v1)), which VOID can implement as a token counter. Training-free early exit (DEER) cut chain-of-thought length 19-80% across 11 models, including Qwen3-1.7B and 4B, with accuracy changing by +0.3 to +5.0, though each confidence probe costs extra decode ([DEER](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.15895v2)). Note the sampling conflict: Qwen recommends temperature 1.0 for thinking, which clashes with VOID's temperature-0 router, so escalation must be a separate call with its own sampler.

Heavier test-time compute is not worth it. At equal token cost on 1.5-7B models, self-refine, forced reflection and self-verified best-of-N lost to plain repeated sampling in all 18 comparisons involving them, and majority vote beat the model's own judged pick by 5-17 points ([Sample More, Reflect Less](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28576)); majority voting can even lower per-problem accuracy on hard questions ([When Self-Consistency Backfires](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.11403)). The striking "1B beats 405B" result depends on a process reward model and many samples on MATH ([arXiv 2502.06703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06703)), which does not transfer to an 8 GB voice loop. Escalation triggers should also avoid model confidence: token entropy in small models is "effectively blind" (under 0.01 bits in 32 of 35 model-dataset combinations) ([arXiv 2609.20824](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20824)). A rule-based or embedding-score trigger is the better bet, and VOID's existing semantic retrieval similarity could serve, though that use is unverified.

For latency, the best-supported pattern is pipeline overlap rather than clever reasoning: stream LLM output to TTS at sentence boundaries, speculate on stable partial transcripts, and keep first audio under roughly 1-2 s ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voice-agent-latency-optimization)). A 300-token think costs about 10 s at 30 tok/s, so the design is: reflex path never thinks; an escalation path plays a prerecorded filler, runs the same Qwen3.5-4B weights with `enable_thinking` and a cap, and speaks the result. Same-weights escalation avoids loading a second model on 8 GB; no source tests this design for local assistants.

## Inference tricks: swap the quant, skip most of the rest

The mixed-precision **Qwen3.5-4B-OptiQ-4bit** is the cheapest model-quality change in the notes. It keeps 75 layers at 8-bit and 173 at 4-bit, chosen by KL sensitivity, and its self-reported card shows MMLU 69.9 vs 68.7, GSM8K 80.5 vs 78.8, and BFCL-V3 simple 90.0 vs 85.0 against uniform 4-bit, for a mean capability score of 68.76 vs 66.86 ([OptiQ card mirror](https://huggingface-proxy-6ci.pages.dev/mlx-community/Qwen3.5-4B-OptiQ-4bit)). These are vendor numbers with small samples, so differences of a point or two are noise, and the card's size is unreconciled: a table says 3.0 or 2.8 GB while the file listing shows 4.02 GB (possibly including a 185 MB MTP head). Check the actual download before adopting; at 4.02 GB it breaks the memory plan. Plain 8-bit is essentially lossless and 6-bit nearly so, but 4-bit at group size 64 costs over 0.5 perplexity on Qwen3-4B, and DWQ recovers roughly 0.5-0.6 effective bits ([n8programs](https://n8programs.substack.com/p/an-examination-of-mlx-quantization)); that measurement is for Qwen3-4B, and I found no DWQ build of Qwen3.5-4B.

MTP corrects an earlier conclusion. mlx-swift-lm 3.32.3 now includes Qwen3.5 MTP speculative decoding (PR #351), separate from the classic draft-model path that fails on Qwen3.5's non-trimmable caches ([3.32.3 release](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/releases/tag/3.32.3)). But the only measurements are from the Python mlx-optiq project on an M4 Pro with 24 GB: Qwen3.5-4B went from 29.2 to 35.0 tok/s greedy (1.20x) and 1.09x with Qwen's sampler, 9B reached 1.32x, and acceptance was 56-72% ([optiq blog](https://mlx-optiq.pages.dev/blog/mtp-on-apple-silicon)). The optiq docs advise skipping MTP at 0.8B and 2B ([optiq docs](https://mlx-optiq.pages.dev/docs/mtp)). The blog also contains a memory trap (a 9B peaked at 16.7 GB before a duplicated-weights fix), so on 8 GB verify that the Swift loader shares base weights. Because voice latency is dominated by time-to-first-token and TTS rather than a 10% decode gain, I rank MTP low. Classic draft models showed mixed results independently (+14-26% for a 9B target on an M2 Ultra, about -25% for self-speculation with a 4B) ([Apple Silicon paper](https://atomgradient.github.io/apple-silicon-llm-inference/paper.pdf)).

Prefill and caching are the larger lever. At about 330 prefill tok/s on a 16 GB M3 (crowd data from the earlier report), every 100 prompt tokens costs 0.3 s, so an 800-token system prompt plus tool list costs about 2.4 s cold. The notes therefore favour keeping the system and tool prefix byte-stable so session cache reuse hits, shrinking the tool list per route, and warming the prefix at launch. 3.32.3 also adds windowed prefill and warm continuation for Qwen3.5, balanced chunking (about 9% faster at 32K), and up to 1.8x faster parallel model loading ([release notes](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-swift-lm/releases/tag/3.32.3)). I found no persistent on-disk prefix cache in the Swift package. KV quantization is skippable: Qwen3.5 has only 8 of 32 layers with full attention, so KV is about 128 MB at 4K tokens (earlier report's arithmetic), and a third-party TurboQuant test slowed Qwen2.5-7B decode by about 28% for an 18% KV saving ([turboquant-mlx](https://github.com/arozanov/turboquant-mlx)), though that is not the Swift implementation.

Apple Foundation Models is a plausible free-memory helper for routing, not for chat. The on-device model is about 3B parameters at 2-bit, with constrained tool calling and guided generation (`@Generable`) ([Apple](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/apple-foundation-models-tech-report-2025)). Apple's docs cite a 4,096-token context on the macOS 26 line, a newer model in macOS 27, and an `MLXLanguageModel` conformer so one `LanguageModelSession` interface can sit over MLX ([Apple updates log](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/updates/foundationmodels.md)); a secondary WWDC summary gives 8,192 for macOS 27 and a 14 Sep 2026 macOS 27 release ([DEV summary](https://dev.to/hariharanjagan/whats-new-in-apples-foundation-models-framework-at-wwdc-2026-5227)). I found no TTFT, tokens per second, memory or thermal figures for an 8 GB M3, so "free" memory is unverified. Apple warns that the model changes with OS updates, so prompts must be re-tested, and VOID must feature-detect availability and fall back to MLX.

## Specialization beats swapping: router, retrieval, LoRA and the ASR step

The strongest evidence points at the router, not the base model. In a voice tool-calling benchmark, parameter filling fell from about 60.6% in English to 39.2% in Indic languages, ground-truth transcripts added at least 24 points on non-Hindi languages, and an Indic-specific ASR recovered 40-55% of the loss ([VoiceAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07978)). That suggests Hinglish errors are largely transcript errors, so alias and phonetic matching against the user's contacts and song vocabulary, plus ASR-noisy training data, likely pay more than a larger LLM. A small whisper-tiny Hinglish fine-tune exists, though no accuracy was retrieved ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/abhishekgautamm/whisper-tiny-hinglish)).

For routing itself, an embedding router is cheap and fast. On MetaTool "similar choices", an outcome-tuned MiniLM-based router reached 83.4% Recall@1 vs 66.4% for static embeddings and published LLM figures of 57-74%, at about 3.5 ms p50 on CPU, though the authors flag that the comparison crosses setups ([OATS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.13426)). VOID's closed intent set is easier than 199 tools, but the cited embedder is English-only, so a multilingual small embedder must be chosen and tested on Hinglish (no number found). The recommended hybrid is to embed the utterance, and when the top-1 margin is high inject only 1-3 tool schemas and examples into the LLM prompt; when it is low, fall back to chat. A fine-tuned DistilBERT also beat four LLMs on a Hinglish sarcasm task, a one-task result ([arXiv 2602.21933](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21933)).

Retrieved few-shot examples are the best-supported prompt lever: a secondary blog aggregating studies reports tool-calling accuracy rising from 16% to 52% with three matched examples and one small-model case from 11% to 75%, with an optimum around k=3-5 and collapse at 8-shot for one model ([Tian Pan](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-12-dynamic-few-shot-retrieval-static-examples-accuracy)). The individual numbers were not checked against the original papers. Retrieved examples go after the static prefix to preserve caching, and memory should be capped at about 100-300 tokens (top 3-5 facts) because Mem0-style retrieval costs about 7K tokens per query on a vendor benchmark ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/library/agent-memory/ai-memory-benchmarks-in-2026)), roughly 20 s of prefill at 330 tok/s.

Constrained decoding needs care. On Qwen3-0.6B through Qwen3-4B, XGrammar lifted schema validity to 100% with 1.6-3.7% throughput cost, but did not fix omitted second calls ([arXiv 2609.23742](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23742)). A conflicting study found a hard answer-only schema dropped a calendar tool task from 91.5% to 48.0% executable accuracy, and recommends reasoning freely and constraining late ([The Constraint Tax](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.26128)). Whether MLX Swift has an XGrammar equivalent is unverified, so the safe default is to validate arguments in code, retry once, and constrain only tool-name fields.

LoRA is promising but unproven on this hardware. FunctionGemma 270M rose from 58% to 85% on Google's mobile-actions task ([Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/models/gemma/functiongemma/)), and a ~960-example tutorial moved tool-name accuracy from 88% to 98%, but on only 50 eval items with an uncontrolled token-limit change ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/tutorial/fine-tune-functiongemma)). A Hinglish chat study raised judged fluency 41% on Qwen2.5-3B using 3,000+ synthetic dialogues, but said models under 3B performed poorly even with good data ([arXiv 2504.19070](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19070v1)). Local training on 8 GB is risky: one user hit instant out-of-memory on Qwen3.5 0.8B and 2B on an 8 GB M1 ([Reddit](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rv1qcv/snapshots/2026-03-16T20%3A46%3A16.316967Z)). The safer path is renting a GPU or using a free T4, then fusing with `mlx_lm.fuse`, which should load in mlx-swift-lm as ordinary weights (unverified), and re-evaluating after re-quantizing to 4-bit ([mlx-lm LORA.md](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/blob/main/mlx_lm/LORA.md)). My extrapolation is 500-1,500 labelled commands including 10-20% "no tool" negatives. Keep persona and router separate, since one runs at temperature 0.7 and the other at 0.

## Ranked action plan

Ranking weighs expected gain, effort and risk, all my judgment from the evidence above; "expected gain" is not a measured number for VOID.

| Rank | Action | Expected gain | Effort | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build a 150-300 command eval set (Hinglish, ASR-noisy, ambiguous, "chat not tool" slices; 30% held out; score tool_id, JSON validity, arguments, p50/p95 latency) | Enables every other decision; ±4 points at n=200 and ~90% accuracy, so ignore gaps under ~5 | Low-medium (days of logging) | Low |
| 2 | Hybrid router: multilingual embedding top-k, inject 1-3 schemas plus 3-5 retrieved examples after a byte-stable prefix, argument validation with one retry | Largest evidenced tool-routing gain (reported +10-40% from examples; unverified for VOID); shorter prompts, better cache hits | Medium | Low-medium (embedder Hinglish quality unknown) |
| 3 | ASR-side fixes: alias and phonetic matching against contacts and songs; ASR-noise augmentation | Transcript errors dominate in Indic voice benchmarks (24+ points) | Low-medium | Low |
| 4 | Swap in Qwen3.5-4B-OptiQ-4bit as deep brain if on-disk size is near 3 GB | +1.9 mean points, +5 BFCL-simple (vendor, small n) | Low (a model ID) | Low-medium (size conflict 3.0 vs 4.02 GB) |
| 5 | A/B LFM3-1.7B as reflex/router in Python mlx-lm first, with thinking off, temp 0.1 and repetition penalty | Possibly large on tool routing and Hinglish (vendor only) | Low for test; high for a Swift port | Medium-high (Swift support absent, unverified claims) |
| 6 | Escalation path: filler, then Qwen3.5-4B thinking with a 100-300 token cap and optional DEER-style exit | Real gain on math and multi-step; little on chat | Medium | Medium (latency, leaky control, separate sampler) |
| 7 | LoRA fine-tune the router on rented GPU, fuse and re-quantize | Potentially large on narrow routing (88% to 98% in a small tutorial) | Medium-high | Medium (data leakage, quantization loss, Swift load unverified) |
| 8 | Apple Foundation Models for classification and `@Generable` argument extraction, with feature detection | Frees MLX memory and time for narrow tasks | Medium | Medium (4K-8K context, OS-dependent behaviour) |
| 9 | MTP speculative decoding on the 4B | 1.09-1.20x on an M4 Pro, likely lower on base M3 | Medium | Medium (memory duplication) |
| Skip | KV quantization, majority voting, self-refine, PRM search, MiniCPM5 (no Hindi), Gemma 4 E2B (3.55 GB, weak tool scores) | None evidenced | n/a | n/a |

Steps 1 to 3 need no new model and should come first; if the router then clears your target, step 5 may be unnecessary. Test LFM3 against the current LFM2 reflex on the eval set before spending Swift-port effort.

## Conflicting numbers and gaps you should know about

Vendor tables disagree on shared baselines, so use only within-table comparisons. Qwen3.5-4B IFBench is 48.40 on the LFM2.5 card but 38.16 on the LFM3 card; its BFCL v4 is 50.56 vs 55.12; Gemma 4 E4B BFCL is 46.39 vs 48.25; and LFM2.5-2.6B IFEval is 95.39 on Liquid's card vs 93.4 on MiniCPM's ([LFM2.5 card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-2.6B), [LFM3 card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B), [MiniCPM5 card](https://huggingface.co/OpenBMB/MiniCPM5-2B)). LFM2.5's numbers are likely thinking-mode results. Other inconsistencies: OptiQ's 3.0/2.8 vs 4.02 GB size; the optiq MTP page's own 9B figures (1.28x vs 1.32x) and its 27B ones (1.36x vs 1.40x); Apple's context window at 4,096 (primary) vs 8,192 (secondary); the two constrained-decoding papers pointing opposite ways; and the mlx-community Gemma 4 page labelling "Gemma license" while Google's card says Apache 2.0.

The gaps are large. Nothing was measured on an 8 GB M3: no tok/s, TTFT, memory, load time or thermals for any model or technique. No Hinglish (romanized, code-mixed) benchmark or user report covers any candidate. LFM3 has no independent evaluation, no Swift support, and no looping or JSON-failure reports (too new). No study quantifies capped thinking, retrieved few-shot, or memory retrieval for tool calling at 2-4B. MLX Swift grammar-constrained decoding, fused-LoRA loading, persistent prefix caching, and ChatSession MTP exposure are all unverified. Several 2026 arXiv preprints cited here were not peer-reviewed checked, and some sources (Reddit snapshots, a Chinese blog, aggregators like BenchLM) are low-confidence.

## Conclusion

The research reframes "better thinking" as better plumbing. The model most likely to beat Qwen3.5-4B on VOID's actual job (calling the right tool, in the right language, fast) is not a bigger or newer thinker but a small instruction-following model with a retrieved, validated prompt, and the only evidence that LFM3 is that model is vendor-reported. Meanwhile the evidence against heavy test-time compute is unusually consistent: self-refine, reflection and voting fail at this scale, and confidence signals from small models are unreliable, so reasoning belongs behind an explicit, capped escalation.

The practical implication is sequencing. An eval set costs days and makes the next six decisions cheap and falsifiable; the router and ASR work likely capture most of the gain regardless of base model; and the model swaps (OptiQ, LFM3) are worth running only as measured A/Bs on your own commands. If LFM3 wins, the real cost is a Swift port, so confirm the win in Python first.
